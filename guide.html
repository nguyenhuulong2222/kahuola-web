How to read the Kahu Ola map

This page explains everything on the hazard map in plain words: what each color and symbol means, where the information comes from, and — just as important — what Kahu Ola does not do. You do not need any technical background. Every term is explained the first time it is used.

In an emergency, call 911 first. Kahu Ola helps you understand what is happening around you. It is not an emergency service and it does not replace official warnings.

Part 1

Quick start — five minutes

Organized by the questions people actually ask, not by feature names.

1. “Is my area safe right now?”

This takes about one minute. On a phone:

  1. Open the Live Map.
  2. At the bottom of the screen, tap the small handle to open Map Details.
  3. Tap Layers & Tools to show the buttons.
  4. Tap Use My Location and allow location when your phone asks.
  5. Read the location line on the Fire Spread Concern card.

On a computer the panel is already open on the right side of the screen, so you can skip steps 2 and 3.

What a calm day looks like

Most days, the answer is reassuring — and it is worth learning what that looks like now, while nothing is happening, so you recognise it later. On a calm day the card shows a low concern level and the map has few or no orange dots. That is the normal state. An empty map is not a broken map; it is good news.

2. “I see smoke or fire — what do I do?”

Call 911 first. Do this before anything else, and before opening any app.

Satellites can take a while to notice a new fire, and they cannot see everything. A person who can see smoke knows more, right now, than any satellite does. Reporting it to 911 is the fastest way to get help moving.

After you have called, the map can help you understand the wider picture — whether other heat has been detected nearby, which way the wind is blowing, and whether the National Weather Service has issued any official warning for your area.

Kahu Ola does not currently accept fire reports from the public. If you see a way to report something in another app, that is not us.

3. “What do the colors and symbols mean?”

This is the decoder. Five things account for almost everything you will see on the map.

Orange dots and bubbles
Places where a satellite detected unusual heat. A bubble with a number means several detections are grouped together at that zoom level — tap it to zoom in and separate them. A dot means heat was seen there; it does not confirm a fire on its own.
Pink and magenta shading
A spread estimate, not a burning area. It shows where fire could travel based on nearby heat detections, wind direction and humidity. Paler pink means lower concern, deep red means the highest. The map legend says it plainly: “Estimate. Shaded areas are not burning.”
E
Orange rings with a letter
Seen when you zoom out to the whole United States. Each ring marks a fire area that has been assessed, and the letter is the concern level: L low, M moderate, H high, E extreme. Tap a ring to fly to that area.
42
Colored numbers
Air quality readings from official monitoring stations. The number is the Air Quality Index and the color is its category — green is Good, yellow is Moderate, orange is Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, and it continues through red, purple and maroon as air gets worse.
One outlined cell
After you tap Use My Location, the single square covering your position is outlined. That one is yours — it is the area the location line on the card is describing.

4. “How fresh is what I’m seeing?”

Nothing on the map is live video. Every number has an age, and the map tells you what it is.

  • Satellite heat detections arrive when a satellite passes overhead. That happens roughly every few hours, and the information reaches us about 20 to 30 minutes after the pass. Between passes, a new fire is invisible to the satellite.
  • Air quality readings are updated about once an hour.
  • Official weather alerts appear as soon as the National Weather Service issues them.

“Updated” and “Latest detection” are different

Updated means the last time Kahu Ola successfully fetched information — it tells you the connection is healthy. Latest detection means the time of the most recent heat the satellite actually saw. A recent “Updated” with an old “Latest detection” is normal and good: it means we checked just now, and there was nothing new to find.

5. What Kahu Ola does not do

This section matters as much as the rest of the guide. Being clear about our limits is how you know when to trust us and when to look elsewhere.

  • We do not issue alerts. Kahu Ola is an independent civic project, not a government agency. We have no authority to order an evacuation or issue a warning.
  • We do not replace official sources. The Hawaiʻi Emergency Management Agency, your County Emergency Management office, and the National Weather Service come first, always. When their guidance differs from anything here, follow theirs.
  • We do not know your identity. There is no account, no sign-up, no password, no email address, and no advertising or tracking software anywhere on this site.
  • Your location never leaves your phone. When you tap Use My Location, your coordinates are compared against the map on your own device. They are never sent to our servers, never saved, and never written into any log.
  • We do not confirm fires. A satellite detection means an instrument measured unusual heat. Only people on the ground confirm what is actually burning.

6. Short answers to common questions

The map looks empty — is it broken?

No. An empty map means no unusual heat has been detected and no official alert is active. That is what most days look like, and it is the answer you want.

Why does the pink shading only appear near fires in the USA view?

At the scale of the whole country, the spread estimate is calculated only around places where satellites actually detected heat. Shading everywhere would suggest we know something about everywhere, and we do not.

Can I read this in Vietnamese?

Yes. This guide is available at kahuola.org/guide-vi, and the map itself has a language selector in the top corner.

Why does the volcano show up as heat?

Because heat is heat — a satellite instrument cannot tell lava from a wildfire. Kahu Ola identifies detections inside the Kīlauea and Mauna Loa volcanic zones and labels them separately, so lava is never presented to you as a wildfire and never feeds the fire-spread estimate.

Part 2

Where the information comes from

Three organisations supply almost everything on this map. Learning them once makes the rest of the guide easy.

NASA FIRMS — the satellites that see heat

NASA satellites carry instruments that measure heat coming off the ground. Several times a day, one of them passes over Hawaiʻi. When it sees a patch of ground that is much hotter than its surroundings, that becomes an orange dot on our map — usually within about half an hour of the satellite passing overhead.

What a detection really is. It is heat that an instrument measured from space. It is not a confirmed fire. It could be a wildfire, but it could also be a lava flow, a large industrial site, or agricultural burning. This is why we say “detection” and never “fire” — the honest word is the one that matches what the instrument did.

Why there are gaps. Satellites orbit; they are not parked above Hawaiʻi. Between one pass and the next, a fire can start and grow completely unseen. This is the single most important limitation of satellite fire detection, and no amount of clever software fixes it. It is also why calling 911 when you see smoke matters so much: you are filling a gap the satellite physically cannot.

Why the volcano appears. A lava lake is extremely hot, so the instrument sees it exactly as it sees a fire. Rather than pretend otherwise, Kahu Ola marks detections inside the Kīlauea and Mauna Loa volcanic zones as volcanic and keeps them out of the wildfire spread estimate entirely.

The instruments are called VIIRS, and they fly on the NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 satellites. You do not need to remember that — but if you ever want to check our work, that is what to search for.

National Weather Service — the official warnings

The National Weather Service, part of NOAA, runs the weather stations across Hawaiʻi and issues the official watches and warnings you see here. When our map shows a Flash Flood warning or a Hurricane advisory, those are the Weather Service’s own words, not ours. We pass them through; we do not write them and we do not edit them.

Where our wind and humidity come from. The fire-spread estimate needs to know which way the wind is blowing and how dry the air is. Those readings come from real Weather Service stations near the area being assessed, and the card names the station it used so you can judge for yourself how close it is.

Observation and forecast are not the same thing. In Hawaiʻi we use measurements from actual nearby stations — what the weather is doing right now. On the United States mainland, station coverage works differently, so we use the Weather Service’s hourly forecast for that grid square instead. A forecast is a prediction, not a measurement, and that is a genuine difference in confidence between the two views.

Learn more at weather.gov →

EPA AirNow and Hawaiʻi Department of Health — the air you breathe

The air-quality numbers are official measurements taken by Hawaiʻi Department of Health monitoring stations and delivered through the EPA’s AirNow system. They update about once an hour.

These are real instruments at real places, not estimates. That also means they only tell you about the air at those specific locations. If the nearest station is on the other side of a ridge from you, conditions where you are standing may genuinely differ — vog in particular can be very local.

Learn more at airnow.gov →

The one distinction that matters: OFFICIAL or ESTIMATE

Everything on this map is one of two things, and every card tells you which by the small line of text above its title.

“Official · [agency name]” means the information was issued by that agency. We are showing you their words. If it says Official · National Weather Service, a meteorologist decided that, not us.

“Estimate · Kahu Ola model” means we calculated it. Our fire-spread estimate takes satellite heat detections, adds wind direction and humidity, and works out where fire could plausibly travel. It is a reasoned guess built from real inputs — useful for awareness, and not a substitute for anything official.

If you remember one thing from this guide, remember to glance at that line before acting on what a card says.

Other sources you will see named

USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory is the authority on Kīlauea and Mauna Loa activity. When detections fall inside a volcanic zone, they are where you should go. PacIOOS supplies Pacific coastal and ocean information. NIFC and county agencies supply official fire perimeters, and NOAA HMS supplies smoke plume analysis.

Part 3

Complete reference — every feature

Every layer, card and control on the map, in the same five-line format. Use the list on the right to jump straight to one.

1 · Fire Signals Official

What it shows
Places where a NASA satellite detected unusual heat in roughly the last day.
Source
NASA FIRMS, delivered through the Kahu Ola server. The panel header reads Fire Signals · Hawaiʻi or Fire Signals · USA depending on your scope.
Symbols & colors
Orange dots are individual detections. Orange bubbles with a number are several detections grouped together — tap to zoom in and separate them. Tapping a dot opens details including how confident the instrument was.
When it’s active, what to do
Detections near you are a reason to pay attention and check for official warnings — not a confirmation that anything is burning. If you can see smoke, call 911.
Honest limits
Satellites pass every few hours, and information arrives 20–30 minutes after a pass. New fires between passes are invisible. Detections are heat, not confirmed fire, and can come from lava or industrial sources.

2 · Fire Spread Concern Estimate

What it shows
Our estimate of where fire could spread from detected heat, shown as shaded squares.
Source
Kahu Ola model, built from NASA FIRMS detections plus National Weather Service wind and humidity. The card kicker reads Estimate · Kahu Ola model.
Symbols & colors
Pale pink through deep red, from Low to Extreme concern. At United States scale it appears instead as orange rings lettered L, M, H or E. After using Use My Location, the square containing you is outlined.
When it’s active, what to do
Treat it as awareness, not instruction. It can tell you that conditions near detected heat favour spread in your direction. It cannot tell you to evacuate — only officials can.
Honest limits
This is a model, not a fire-behaviour forecast. Shaded areas are not burning. Some islands have no nearby weather station, so their squares are scored on distance alone — the card says so when that applies.

3 · Fire Weather Official

What it shows
Areas where weather conditions make fire spread more dangerous — dry air, strong wind, or both.
Source
National Weather Service and RAWS remote weather stations.
Symbols & colors
Shaded zones on the map, with a status of Normal, Watch, High or Red Flag. Toggle it with the Fire Weather button.
When it’s active, what to do
A Red Flag condition means officials consider fire danger critical. Avoid anything that could cause a spark, and follow National Weather Service guidance.
Honest limits
Conditions are measured at stations and applied to the surrounding area. Hawaiʻi terrain changes quickly, so wind in your valley may differ from the nearest station’s reading.

4 · Smoke Official

What it shows
Smoke plumes identified from satellite imagery by human analysts.
Source
NOAA Hazard Mapping System (HMS).
Symbols & colors
Translucent grey areas over the map. Toggle with the Smoke Layer button.
When it’s active, what to do
Smoke overhead can affect air quality even when a fire is far away. If you have asthma or a heart condition, check the Air Quality card and consider staying indoors.
Honest limits
Analysis is produced periodically, not continuously, and shows smoke seen from above. High-altitude smoke may not affect the air at ground level at all.

5 · Fire Perimeters Official

What it shows
The mapped outline of a fire’s edge, as recorded by fire agencies.
Source
NIFC and county fire agencies.
Symbols & colors
Outlined shapes around affected land. Toggle with the Perimeters button.
When it’s active, what to do
A perimeter is the most authoritative fire information on the map — it was drawn by people working the incident. Follow instructions from the responding agency.
Honest limits
Perimeters are updated when agencies publish them, which can lag an active fire by hours. An out-of-date perimeter may be smaller than the current fire.

6 · Flash Flood Official

What it shows
Active flash flood watches and warnings for Hawaiʻi.
Source
National Weather Service. The card kicker reads Official · National Weather Service.
Symbols & colors
A prominent card at the top of the panel, plus affected areas marked on the map. Rain radar switches on automatically when a warning is active.
When it’s active, what to do
Follow the National Weather Service’s instructions exactly. Never drive through moving water — it is the most common cause of flood deaths.
Honest limits
We show alerts as issued. Flooding can begin before a warning is published, especially in steep terrain where water arrives within minutes.

7 · Flood Context Estimate

What it shows
Terrain that tends to channel or collect water, shown only while an official flood alert is active.
Source
A hybrid: the National Weather Service alert decides whether it appears, and the terrain shapes are Kahu Ola’s own. Because the shapes are ours, it is tagged Estimate.
Symbols & colors
Blue-toned terrain polygons beneath the alert areas.
When it’s active, what to do
Use it to understand where water is likely to run during an alert that is already in force. Act on the official alert, not on this layer.
Honest limits
Terrain context is general, not a flood map. It does not predict depth, timing, or whether a specific property will flood.

8 · Rain Radar Official

What it shows
Where rain is falling right now, from weather radar.
Source
NOAA radar tiles.
Symbols & colors
Green through red overlay, where stronger colors mean heavier rain. Off by default in Hawaiʻi; switches on automatically during a flood warning and in the USA view.
When it’s active, what to do
Use it to see whether heavy rain is heading toward you during a flood alert.
Honest limits
Radar sees precipitation aloft, which is not always what reaches the ground. Mountains block radar beams, so some valleys are poorly covered.

9 · Rain Totals Official

What it shows
How much rain has accumulated over recent hours.
Source
NEXRAD radar-derived precipitation estimates (QPE).
Symbols & colors
Shaded accumulation areas. The panel labels the source as live or unavailable.
When it’s active, what to do
High totals on already-saturated ground raise the risk of flash flooding and landslides, particularly on burn scars.
Honest limits
When the upstream feed is down the panel reads NEXRAD QPE · Unavailable — that is an honest statement that no data arrived, not a reading of zero rain. Radar-derived totals are estimates and can differ from a rain gauge.

10 · Wind, Heat & Other Alerts Official

What it shows
Official National Weather Service alerts that are not flood, tsunami or hurricane — high wind, heat, and similar.
Source
National Weather Service. Card kicker: Official · National Weather Service.
Symbols & colors
A card listing active alerts, with affected zones marked on the map.
When it’s active, what to do
Read the alert and follow the National Weather Service’s instructions. These are their words.
Honest limits
Alerts cover official zones, which are larger than a neighbourhood. An alert for your zone does not mean every part of it is equally affected.

11 · Hurricane Official

What it shows
Active tropical cyclone advisories affecting the region.
Source
National Hurricane Center. Card kicker: Official · National Hurricane Center.
Symbols & colors
A card showing the current advisory. Appears when an advisory is active or in the USA view.
When it’s active, what to do
Follow the National Hurricane Center and your county’s emergency management instructions. Do not wait for a Kahu Ola update to act.
Honest limits
We show advisories as published. Forecast tracks change; always check the Hurricane Center directly for the latest position.

12 · Tsunami Official

What it shows
Tsunami watches, advisories and warnings for Hawaiʻi.
Source
NWS Tsunami Warning Center. Card kicker: Official · NWS Tsunami Warning Center.
Symbols & colors
A card showing the current status. Appears when an alert is active or in the USA view.
When it’s active, what to do
Move inland and to higher ground immediately, following your county’s evacuation routes. Do not use this map to decide whether to evacuate — use the sirens and official instructions.
Honest limits
For a locally generated tsunami, natural warnings — strong shaking, or the ocean suddenly withdrawing — arrive before any official message. Do not wait for a screen.

13 · Coastal & Surf Official

What it shows
High surf, coastal flood and beach hazard statements.
Source
National Weather Service, with Pacific ocean context from PacIOOS.
Symbols & colors
A card titled Coastal & Surf listing active statements.
When it’s active, what to do
Stay off exposed rocks and beaches during high surf, and follow lifeguard and county guidance.
Honest limits
Statements are issued by shoreline region. Conditions vary sharply between individual beaches within the same region.

14 · Landslide Estimate

What it shows
Terrain where heavy rain raises the chance of slope failure, shown while flood conditions are active.
Source
Kahu Ola terrain context. There is no official agency issuing landslide warnings for these areas, which is exactly why this is tagged Estimate.
Symbols & colors
A card describing terrain risk, with a low-risk state when nothing is elevated.
When it’s active, what to do
On steep ground during heavy rain, be alert for debris on roads and for changes in the sound of running water. Report blocked roads to local authorities.
Honest limits
This is general terrain reasoning, not a geological assessment. It cannot tell you whether a particular slope will fail.

15 · Local Hazards Official

What it shows
Locally issued statements and bulletins that do not fit the other categories.
Source
NWS Honolulu, via the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive.
Symbols & colors
Listed within the panel’s secondary sections.
When it’s active, what to do
Read the statement — it is written by the local forecast office for local conditions.
Honest limits
Coverage depends on what the local office publishes and on the archive being reachable.

16 · Air Quality & Vog Official

What it shows
Measured air quality at official monitoring stations, including sulphur dioxide from volcanic vog.
Source
Hawaiʻi Department of Health monitors delivered through EPA AirNow. Card kicker: Official · Hawaiʻi DOH via EPA AirNow.
Symbols & colors
Colored dots with the Air Quality Index number inside: green Good, yellow Moderate, orange Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, then red, purple and maroon as it worsens. Toggle with the AQI Layer button.
When it’s active, what to do
At orange and above, people with asthma, heart or lung conditions, kūpuna and young children should limit time outdoors. During vog episodes, close windows on the upwind side.
Honest limits
Readings update hourly and describe the air at the monitor, not at your door. Vog is highly local — a ridge between you and the station can mean very different air.

17 · Fire Scope — Hawaiʻi or USA Control

What it shows
Switches the whole map between Hawaiʻi and the continental United States.
Source
Kahu Ola control. Labelled Hawaiʻi and USA.
Symbols & colors
The panel header changes to match, and in the USA view the spread estimate appears as lettered orange rings rather than shaded squares.
When it’s active, what to do
Use Hawaiʻi for daily awareness. Use USA to follow mainland fire seasons or check on family elsewhere.
Honest limits
In the USA view, wind and humidity come from hourly forecasts rather than nearby station measurements, and the spread estimate is calculated only around detected heat.

18 · Satellite Basemap Control

What it shows
Swaps the plain map background for satellite photography.
Source
Kahu Ola control, using map imagery. Button labelled Satellite.
Symbols & colors
The background becomes aerial imagery; all hazard layers stay exactly where they were.
When it’s active, what to do
Useful for recognising real landmarks — a gulch, a subdivision, a stretch of highway — when deciding how close something is to you.
Honest limits
Satellite imagery is archive photography, often months or years old. It shows the landscape as it was, not as it is today.

19 · Use My Location Control

What it shows
Centres the map on you and adds a line to the Fire Spread Concern card about your specific square.
Source
Your device’s own location service. Button labelled Use My Location.
Symbols & colors
The map centres on your position and the square containing you is outlined.
When it’s active, what to do
This is the fastest way to answer “what about right here?” Your phone will ask permission the first time; you can decline and still use every other part of the map.
Honest limits
Your coordinates are compared with the map on your own device and never sent to our servers, never stored and never logged. Because the comparison is local, we could not identify you even if we wanted to. If the spread data is unavailable, the card says so rather than guessing.

20 · The Phone Sheet and Layers & Tools Control

What it shows
On a phone, the panel slides up from the bottom of the screen so the map stays the main view.
Source
Kahu Ola interface. The handle reads Map Details; inside it, Layers & Tools holds the buttons.
Symbols & colors
Tap the handle to open the panel. The hazard information appears first; tap Layers & Tools to reveal the buttons, and tap it again to hide them.
When it’s active, what to do
The buttons start hidden on purpose, so what is happening around you is what you see first. Open them when you want to change layers.
Honest limits
On a computer this panel is always visible on the right, so there is nothing to open.
